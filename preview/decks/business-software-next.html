Business software is next.

About 45 seconds. Now look at the hands that stayed down at the start. The people who don't write code, but know exactly what software they need. Every organisation has thousands of them, and they are angry. Not at us. At waiting three months for IT to build a form.

01
A suite is not a workspace.

The distinction sounds subtle. It decides who can win.

About 15 seconds. First the distinction everything else rests on.

You buy a suite. You build on a workspace.

A workspace

  • One identity layer: log in once.
  • One permission model across every app.
  • One data layer underneath.
  • The apps can come from anywhere.
  • Each app keeps its own community and roadmap.

A suite

  • A box of predetermined tools.
  • One vendor builds every one of them.
  • The same vendor wires them together.
  • You pay for the bundle.
  • Whatever is missing waits in their backlog.
The second model is where open source has an advantage no closed vendor can match.

About 90 seconds. Start on the right. A suite is a box. One vendor builds the tools, wires them together and charges you for the bundle. If you need something they did not build, you wait.

Now the left. A workspace shares three things underneath: one identity, one permission model, one data layer. On top of that, the applications can come from anywhere. That is the line to land: the apps can come from anywhere. A closed vendor cannot say that sentence. Open source can.

A platform is when the economic value of everybody that uses it exceeds the value of the company that creates it.

Bill Gates, as recalled by Chamath Palihapitiya · via Ben Thompson, Stratechery, 2018

About 45 seconds. Read it slowly. This is the test. By this definition a suite is never a platform: all the value sits with the vendor. A workspace can become one, the moment the people building on it create more value than the company underneath. That is what WordPress did. Fair disclosure: this is a recollection of something Gates said, not a transcript.

About 90 seconds. This is not a plan, it runs today. Walk the blocks left to right. Nextcloud is the ground: files, mail, calendar, chat, one login and one permission model. Office comes from several communities at once: LibreOffice and OpenOffice on the desktop, Collabora and now Euro-Office in the browser. Euro-Office is the new one, a fork of OnlyOffice by Nextcloud and IONOS, with XWiki and OpenProject among its partners.

Then OpenProject, XWiki and Matrix: independent projects with their own communities and roadmaps. Nobody at Nextcloud builds them, and yet they run as first-class citizens inside an open workspace. The German government's openDesk combines exactly these, and release 1.18 came out in August.

Land the advantage: what a closed vendor has to build and maintain at enormous cost, the open workspace gets from communities that were already there. That compounds. A vendor's R&D budget does not.

If asked about Euro-Office: OnlyOffice objected to the fork and ended its partnership with Nextcloud. Nextcloud says the attribution notices have since been added. Don't claim the dispute is settled.

Office is getting solved in the open. Business software is not.

Different in every organisation

About 60 seconds. Draw the line clearly, because the previous slide blurs it. Office software is documents, mail, calendar and chat. It is the same for every organisation, so one good answer fits almost everyone. That fight is being won in the open right now: LibreOffice, Collabora, Euro-Office, openDesk, Schleswig-Holstein.

Business software is the right-hand side. The CRM, the books, case handling, HR, the leave-request form. It is different in every organisation, it is the long tail, and nobody is going to build all of it for you. That is year three: once you have replaced Microsoft 365, the real question is where your actual work runs. This is the side that has not been wilded yet, and it is exactly where Microsoft points Power Platform.

Here is why it matters. For thirty years the argument was: Microsoft has more engineers, so Microsoft has more features. That math no longer holds. Features will be built by end users with AI assistants. So the side with the most people building wins. Not the side with the biggest product team.

This week Microsoft offered 30% of its staff money to leave.

Its reasoning: AI builds the software now. Ours: so do your users. They are already angry, they work for free, and none of them asked for a buyout.

Fortune · CNBC · 26 October 2026

About 45 seconds. Deliver it dry, then wait for the laugh. Read the headline. Pause. "Their reasoning is that AI builds the software now." Pause. "Ours is that your users do." Then the lede.

Get the facts right if anyone checks: on 26 October 2026 Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 people, roughly 27 to 30 percent of its 223,000 staff, as part of its AI push. These are buyouts, not layoffs, and nobody knows yet how many will accept. Say "offered money to leave", never "fired".

Then the serious point: they are betting their workforce on AI building the software. We bet on the people who use it.

The door is closing on the fastest-moving segment.

Under the US CLOUD Act a US provider can be compelled to hand over data, wherever it is stored. That legal risk keeps sensitive government work off US clouds.

  • Microsoft France, under oath in the French Senate, June 2025: it "cannot guarantee" otherwise.
  • The Dutch parliament asked in March 2025 for a government cloud and an exit strategy.
  • Schleswig-Holstein moved 40,000 mailboxes off Exchange in October 2025.
  • The International Criminal Court's prosecutor lost his Microsoft mailbox after US sanctions.

About 90 seconds. Careful with the wording here. The CLOUD Act does not forbid any government from buying Microsoft. What it does is let US authorities demand data from a US provider, wherever the data sits. Microsoft France admitted under oath it cannot guarantee that will never happen to French public data, while adding it never has so far.

That risk is enough. Governments are moving: the Dutch motions, Schleswig-Holstein, the ICC. So for their own workflows, the long tail of forms and small apps, European governments are the one segment where Power Platform's door is closing. And it is the segment moving fastest.

03
Trust and security.

IT will only say yes when a citizen-built app cannot break the basics.

About 15 seconds. First: trust. Why would IT ever let Yusuf build an app?

The basics come from the framework, not from each app.

Every citizen-built app gets the hard parts for free, built once and tested once.

  • Login, permissions and the audit trail come from the workspace.
  • Every write is validated against the data model, in the form and again in the API.
  • Lists, forms, search and filters are shared components, accessible by default.
  • A security fix in the framework reaches every app at once.

About 60 seconds. When every app codes its own login check, its own permissions and its own forms, every app carries its own bugs. That is why IT says no to citizen-built apps, and they are right to.

So take the basics out of the app. Identity, permissions and the audit trail come from Nextcloud. Validation runs twice: in the browser and in the API, so even a script or an AI agent cannot store a broken record. Lists and forms are shared components. Fix one and all 21 of our apps get the fix. What is left for the citizen to build is the part only they know: what the app is for. Remember the 497 holes from the start. In WordPress every plugin carries its own security. Here the citizen-built app carries almost none: the framework does.

Buildiq

About 75 seconds. The trick is that the app is not code. It is one JSON file, a manifest, that says what the app is: this list, from this data, with these columns. One shared runtime draws every app from it.

That is what makes AI safe here. When AI writes code for you, you own a pile of code nobody reviewed. In Buildiq the AI writes a manifest. You review the change, you approve it, and it is validated before it runs. The tools act with your own permissions, and every step lands in the audit trail. Enough slides. Let me show you.

Tailor any app, keep a common core.

Common core

  • From the app store
  • Maintained once, for everyone
  • Updates and security fixes

Your changes

  • Extra fields, pages and rules
  • Made by admins and users
  • Stored as the difference only

Your app

  • Fits your organisation exactly
  • Keeps receiving updates
  • No fork to maintain
This is the WordPress move again: a small common core, and everyone's own layer on top.

About 60 seconds. The same works for apps that already exist. An admin adds a field or a page and saves. We store only the difference from the app that shipped. When version two arrives from the app store, the difference lands on top again. You keep your changes and you keep getting updates. For government this is the big one: one common app, maintained once, and every municipality tailors it without becoming its own island.

Where it stops, honestly.

  • Kanban boards, checkout screens and wizards are still custom code.
  • Our own CRM still has 53 custom pages out of 104.
  • Integrations and heavy business logic stay in PHP.
  • We hold ourselves to one rule: the number of custom pages per app may only go down.

About 45 seconds. Don't skip this, the room will trust the rest more. Manifests don't remove all code. Pipelinq still has 53 custom pages out of 104. That is not a success story yet. What we can promise is the direction: custom pages must explain themselves, and their number may only go down.
04
A platform and a store.

An app nobody can find helps one organisation. An app in a store helps all of them, and pays the people who made it.

About 15 seconds. Second: the store. Trust lets them start. The store makes it worth finishing.

Organisations need to find what others already built.

600
apps in the store today
The Nextcloud app store, roughly. Install in two minutes from inside Nextcloud.
6,000
apps, the store's goal
Ten times more, through the partner programme Nextcloud launched in July 2026.
1
place to look
Find it, install it, tailor it, and keep receiving its updates.
Today a good municipal app stays in a drawer, and the next municipality builds it again.

About 60 seconds. This is the WordPress lesson again. The plugin directory made sixty thousand plugins findable. Without it, every site would have built its own contact form.

Government today has no such directory for small apps. A municipality builds a great intake form and it stays in a drawer. Nextcloud wants to grow its store from 600 to 6,000 apps. Those 6,000 will not come from 6,000 more PHP developers. They come from the angry citizens, if the store accepts what they build.

The store pays developers. Who pays the idea?

The developer

  • Sells, hosts or supports apps
  • Finally a market for open-source apps
  • A model that works

The idea

  • A feature idea, a workflow design
  • Often the most valuable part
  • Almost no model at all

The citizen coder

  • A working prototype, built with AI
  • Not traditional code
  • No model either
Users are the moat. If the citizen-developer wave means anything, they need a reason to contribute.

About 60 seconds. A store gives developers what open-source apps never had: a place to sell, host and support what they build. That part we know how to do.

But open source has almost no model for the person who brings the idea, designs the workflow, or builds a working prototype with an AI assistant. Yet after this week's Microsoft news, that is exactly the person who will build most of the software. Users are the moat. Not the code, not the features: the people who trust the platform enough to build on it.
IP

A stake, not a donation.

Assign the IP of a contributed feature to the people who submit it, developer or not.

  • A feature idea, a workflow design or an AI-built prototype all count.
  • The contributor holds a stake in what they brought.
  • The software stays open source for everyone.
  • This is a test, not a policy. Tell us where it breaks.

About 75 seconds. One idea we are testing: the IP of a contributed feature goes to whoever submitted it, developer or not. Not a donation to a project, but a stake in it. The code stays open, the person who brought the feature owns a piece of it.

Be ready for the legal question. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, so an idea on its own cannot be "owned" as IP. A stake for a non-developer has to be arranged by agreement: a share, a revenue split, a cooperative. Nextcloud itself uses the DCO, which transfers no rights at all, whereas a CLA does. Say: "That is exactly why it is a test. How would you structure it?"
